
Abstract

This paper looks at how three measures of UK income distribution, the Gini coefficient, the S80/S20 ratio and the at-risk-of-poverty threshold, changed between 2005 and 2018, all taken from Eurostat. It also checks whether there's a statistical relationship between inequality and the poverty threshold over this time. An indexed time series and a multiple linear regression are used for this. The S80/S20 ratio turned out to have a significant positive relationship with the poverty threshold, but the Gini coefficient didn't once S80/S20 was added in. This result should be treated carefully though, since the poverty threshold is worked out directly from median income, so some of the link between it and inequality might just be down to how the numbers are built rather than any real economic connection. The results are also looked at alongside the 2008 financial crisis, austerity after 2010, and the 2016 referendum, to think about what the data can and can't really tell us, and the paper finishes by setting out some ways the analysis could be made stronger in future.

Introduction

Inequality and poverty get talked about together a lot but they're measured differently and don't always move the same way. Someone can be poor in absolute terms even in a fairly equal society, and a very unequal society can still have a falling poverty rate if everyone's income is going up, just unevenly. That's part of why this paper treats them as two separate things worth comparing rather than assuming one automatically tells you about the other.

This paper looks at three UK indicators from Eurostat between 2005 and 2018: the Gini coefficient, the S80/S20 ratio and the at-risk-of-poverty threshold. First it looks at how these moved compared to each other over the period, using an indexed chart so they can actually be compared even though they're on totally different scales. Then it tests whether there's a statistical relationship between the inequality measures and the threshold, using correlation and then a small regression model, while being honest about what that relationship can and can't actually show, since correlation between economic series over time is very easy to find and not always meaningful. The period chosen, 2005 to 2018, isn't random either, it covers calm years before the financial crisis, the crisis and its aftermath, the austerity period, and the years following the Brexit vote, so it's a reasonable stretch of time to see whether these measures track each other consistently or whether the relationship changes depending on what's happening in the wider economy.

Data and Definitions

All three series come from Eurostat for the UK, covering 2005 to 2018 every year, which means fourteen data points for each variable. Using one source for all three avoids the problem of mixing data built on different definitions, which happens if you pull inequality numbers from one place and poverty numbers from another.
Gini coefficient - measures inequality across the whole income distribution, from 0 (completely equal, everyone has the same income) to 100 (one person has everything). It's probably the most commonly used inequality measure in economics because it summarises the entire distribution in a single number, but that's also its weakness, since it can hide what's actually happening at different points of the distribution.
S80/S20 ratio - compares the income of the richest 20% to the poorest 20%. Unlike Gini it ignores the middle of the distribution completely and only reacts to what's happening at the two ends. So if the middle 60% of earners don't change much but the top and bottom move apart, S80/S20 picks that up clearly while Gini might barely move.
At-risk-of-poverty threshold - Eurostat defines this as 60% of the national median equivalised disposable income after social transfers. It's a money value, not a percentage, so it goes up or down whenever the median income does. Because it's relative rather than fixed, it's a measure of relative poverty rather than an absolute poverty line, meaning the threshold itself moves even if nobody's actual circumstances change, just because the national median shifts.

Methodology

Each series was indexed to 2005 as the base year (2005 = 100) so they could be compared visually even though they're measured in different units, Gini as a number out of 100, S80/S20 as a ratio, and the threshold in pounds. Indexing just means dividing every year's value by the 2005 value and multiplying by 100, so all three lines start at the same point and you can see relative change side by side. A line chart was made in R with ggplot2 to show this. Two methods were used to test for a relationship between the inequality measures and the threshold: Pairwise correlation, to see how each inequality measure relates to the threshold on its own, without controlling for the other variable. Multiple linear regression, modelling the threshold using both Gini and S80/S20 together, to see if each one adds something once the other is controlled for, since they're likely to be correlated with each other as well as with the threshold. It's worth saying early on, rather than burying it near the end, that this regression doesn't prove inequality causes changes in the poverty line. The threshold is just a fixed share of median income, and Gini and S80/S20 come from the same income data, so some relationship between them is basically expected regardless of any real economic cause. So the regression is really just showing how closely each inequality measure tracks the median, not proving a causal link, and that distinction matters a lot for how the results should be read.

Results

Model R²: 0.606, so roughly 61% of the variation in the threshold is linked to Gini and S80/S20 together, leaving about 39% unexplained by these two variables, which is a reasonable amount given how small the sample is and how many other things could be affecting the threshold. Overall model p-value: 0.00595, significant at the 5% level, which means the model as a whole is unlikely to be picking up a relationship that's purely due to chance. Gini p-value: 0.175, not significant once S80/S20 is in the model, so on its own Gini doesn't add much once the other variable is accounted for. S80/S20 p-value: 0.00398, a significant positive relationship, clearly the stronger of the two predictors in this model. So S80/S20 kept its significant positive link to the threshold, but Gini lost its significance once both were in the model together. The threshold itself rose from about £11,117 in 2005 to £12,168 in 2018, an increase of roughly 9.5% over the thirteen years, which is a fairly modest rise once you consider inflation over the same period. Meanwhile Gini and S80/S20 moved along their own separate paths, with neither rising or falling in a perfectly straight line, as shown in the indexed chart, which makes year-by-year comparison more useful than just comparing the start and end points.

Analysis

5.1 The trend against real events

Numbers like this only really make sense next to what was actually happening in the economy at the time, since statistics don't exist in a vacuum. 2005-2018 covers three big UK events that probably affected these series in different ways. The 2008 crash hit top incomes hard, bonuses, capital income, self-employment profits all fell sharply as financial markets collapsed, which would compress measured inequality for a bit since the people at the very top lost more, proportionally, than everyone else. Since the threshold tracks the median and not the mean, it wouldn't move as much from a shock mainly hitting high earners, because the median is far less sensitive to what happens at the extreme top of the distribution than an average would be. So a drop in Gini around 2009-2010 next to a fairly steady threshold fits with a crisis that hit the top more than it helped the bottom, which is a point worth making since a falling Gini is sometimes wrongly read as good news for low earners when it might just reflect bad news for high earners.
After 2010, austerity policies, benefit freezes, tax credit cuts, public sector pay freezes, hit lower income households the hardest, since those households rely more heavily on benefits and tax credits as a share of total income. Since these changes act directly on the bottom of the distribution, they'd probably show up more clearly in S80/S20, which only looks at the top and bottom fifths, than in Gini, which can stay fairly steady if the middle of the distribution doesn't move much even while the bottom falls behind. That fits well with S80/S20 having the stronger statistical link to the threshold in this analysis, since cuts concentrated at the bottom would pull the S80/S20 ratio up noticeably. After 2016, the referendum and the resulting fall in the value of the pound pushed up import prices, since the UK imports a lot of its food and goods, and this would slow real income growth generally across most of the distribution, including at the median, which would also slow growth in the threshold since it's based directly on the median. This period is a useful reminder that external shocks to prices can affect these measures even without any change in how income is actually distributed between rich and poor.

5.2 Gini vs S80/S20

The fact that S80/S20 was significant and Gini wasn't isn't just a coincidence, there's a real conceptual reason behind it that's worth spelling out. Gini reacts to the whole distribution and can stay flat even if the top and bottom move apart, as long as the middle balances it out in some way, because it's calculated from the area between the actual income distribution and a hypothetical line of perfect equality across everyone. S80/S20 only looks at the top and bottom 20%, so it reacts more directly and more sensitively to the same groups that actually determine whether the median, and so the threshold, moves up or down. Basically, over 2005-2018 the poverty threshold moved more in line with the gap between the richest and poorest than with overall inequality across the whole population, which suggests the changes happening in this period were concentrated at the extremes of the income distribution rather than spread evenly across everyone. This matters for policy too, in a general sense, since it suggests that measures aimed at the very top or very bottom of the income scale might have more visible effects on poverty measures than broad, economy-wide approaches that try to shift the whole distribution evenly.

5.3 The built-in relationship problem

The biggest issue with this whole paper, and probably the most important thing to understand before taking the results seriously, is that all three numbers come from the same survey data, same country, same year. So some of the relationship between them could just be because of how they're calculated, not because of any real economic link between inequality and poverty as separate phenomena. If income gets more concentrated at the top in one year, S80/S20 and the median can both shift in that same survey round without there needing to be any separate cause-and-effect relationship between "inequality" and "poverty" at all. So this regression is more just showing things moving together within the same underlying data, not proving that inequality actually drives the poverty line in any independent sense.

5.4 Other limitations

Reverse causality is possible too, the model treats the threshold as the result and inequality as the cause, but it could just as easily be argued the other way round, that changes in median income are what's really driving the measured inequality statistics. That's just a choice made for the purposes of this model, not something that's actually been proven one way or the other. Other things were left out that probably matter a lot, like GDP growth, unemployment, inflation and tax-benefit policy changes, all of which could plausibly be affecting both the inequality measures and the median income at the same time, independently of each other. So some of that 0.606 R² might just be these other background factors showing up indirectly through the inequality variables, rather than reflecting a genuine relationship between inequality and poverty themselves. Gini and S80/S20 are also built from overlapping underlying data, since they're both calculated from the same household income distribution, so Gini losing significance once S80/S20 is added might just be multicollinearity, meaning the two variables are too similar to separate out their individual effects properly, rather than reflecting anything meaningful about which one actually matters more. There's also only 14 yearly data points here, which isn't a lot at all for a two-variable regression model, so this result probably wouldn't hold up under a stricter significance test, a larger sample, or if corrections were made for the fact that several different relationships were tested across this paper.

5.5 Data quality

Eurostat's UK data comes from survey infrastructure similar to EU-SILC, which means the usual survey problems apply, sampling error, non-response, and under-representation of very high earners, who are less likely to respond to household surveys or may under-report their true income. The equivalisation process, adjusting household income for household size and composition so that a family of four isn't compared directly to a single person, involves methodological choices that can shift both measured inequality and the poverty threshold without any real change happening in actual living standards or incomes. It's also worth noting that any revisions Eurostat makes to its methodology over time, or any changes in how the underlying UK surveys are conducted, could introduce small breaks in these series that aren't visible just from looking at the numbers, which is another reason to treat small year-to-year movements with some caution rather than reading too much into them.

Conclusion

There are a few different levels of confidence in what this paper actually shows, and it's worth separating them out clearly rather than treating every finding as equally solid. Strongly supported: UK inequality and the at-risk-of-poverty threshold both changed measurably over 2005-2018, and S80/S20 specifically is statistically linked to movements in the threshold, a result that held up even after accounting for Gini in the same model. More weakly supported: change over this period happened mostly at the extremes of the income distribution, as captured by S80/S20, rather than being spread evenly across the whole distribution, which is what the Gini coefficient would have picked up more clearly if that had been the case. Not supported: that inequality actually caused the threshold to rise or fall in any independent sense. Since the threshold comes directly from median income, and the inequality measures are calculated from the same underlying income distribution, this could easily just be the same numbers moving together within one shared dataset rather than one genuinely causing the other. Both series were, in any case, probably shaped by the same external events, the 2008 financial crash, austerity after 2010, and the inflation that followed the 2016 referendum. Future work on this topic could add macroeconomic controls like GDP growth, unemployment and CPI inflation into the regression, use the at-risk-of-poverty rate instead of the threshold as a genuinely separate outcome variable rather than one built from the same median, and extend the time series further back or forward to get more data points and a more reliable statistical result overall.
